Research Finding
The Problem We Found

Personal care products are still designed for visual certainty.

For visually impaired users, the bathroom can turn a familiar routine into a high-friction task because bottles, tubes, labels, colors, and brand cues often assume that sight is available.

Design insight

The bathroom can become a tactile desert.

In wet environments, touch becomes less reliable. Water, soap, steam, low contrast, slippery surfaces, and similar bottle shapes make product identification harder exactly when the user needs confidence and speed.

Without clear non-visual cues, users often rely on memory, habit, and guesswork. That uncertainty can increase stress, reduce confidence, create dependency on caregivers, and affect dignity during private hygiene routines.

01 Product confusion

Shampoo, conditioner, lotion, cleanser, and similar products can share almost identical silhouettes, pumps, and bottle weights once visual branding is removed.

02 Wet-hand interaction

Water, soap residue, and steam reduce tactile accuracy, making labels, textures, and small markings harder to interpret in the moment.

03 Cognitive load

Users may need to memorize product locations, sequence steps, and track what has already been used while also managing a slippery and changing environment.

04 Privacy and dignity loss

When routines depend on another person to identify, arrange, or confirm products, private care can become less independent and emotionally heavier.

Research
Research Insights

What the research showed about bathroom accessibility.

Our design research pointed to a clear pattern: the bathroom is not just another room. It is wet, private, time-sensitive, and full of products that are often designed to be recognized visually.

High-risk environment

Bathrooms are difficult spaces for visually impaired users because water, hard surfaces, low contrast, and slippery movement all increase the cost of small mistakes.

Common tools break down

Smartphones, paper labels, and voice-only tools can be unreliable when hands are wet, steam is present, or the shower and fan make the room noisy.

Sensory guesswork

Users may need to distinguish products that feel almost the same, especially when bottles share similar shapes, pumps, weights, or textures.

Repeated small assistance

Caregivers often help with small tasks like product setup, checking labels, or confirming what is available, which can reduce privacy over time.

Hybrid support is more practical

A system that combines physical docking, tactile feedback, audio cues, and sensing is more reliable than depending on only one technology.

Strategy
Design Principles

Five rules that keep the product accessible, durable, and dignified.

These principles guide how Anchor-Sense should behave in the bathroom: quiet enough to feel natural, clear enough to reduce uncertainty, and refined enough to belong in a modern home.

Non-visual first

The system should work without relying on sight. Core actions must be understandable through placement, touch, vibration, and audio.

Minimal interaction

Users should not need complex controls in the bathroom. The product should respond to normal actions like placing, lifting, and returning products.

Multi-sensory feedback

Haptic and audio cues should support each other, so confirmation remains clear when the room is wet, noisy, steamy, or visually difficult.

Durability first

The device must survive water, steam, soap, humidity, and repeated daily handling without feeling fragile or high-maintenance.

Dignity by design

The product should feel premium, calm, and non-clinical, supporting independence without making the bathroom feel like a medical space.

The strategy is simple: reduce uncertainty while preserving the privacy and atmosphere of a personal bathroom routine.

Core Technology

Quiet hardware, clear intelligence.

Anchor-Sense works like a small bathroom-native sensing system: a controller reads the dock, understands what changed, and responds with feedback that does not depend on sight.

Embedded Control UnitAn ESP32 or similar microcontroller acts as the system brain, coordinating sensors, product states, feedback events, and optional wireless updates.
Weight Sensing DockLoad cells or weight sensors under each slot estimate product removal, return, and remaining quantity without asking the user to read a label.
Product IdentificationRFID/NFC tags or fixed dock-slot mapping help the system know whether the item is shampoo, conditioner, cleanser, lotion, or another hygiene product.
Non-visual FeedbackA haptic motor and short audio prompts confirm selection, wrong placement, low supply, or a completed action in a way that works with wet hands.
Optional Safety LayerFloor, motion, or impact sensing can add fall-aware alerts for bathrooms, assisted-living suites, and other safety-sensitive environments.
Bathroom-ready BuildBattery design, sealed charging, waterproof enclosure choices, drainage, and anti-slip materials are considered so the dock fits wet daily use.

How It Works

A simple interaction loop turns product placement and use into clear, non-visual confirmation.

  1. Product is placed in dockEach bottle or object returns to a known slot in the bathroom tray.
  2. System recognizes position and identityThe controller reads slot position, weight profile, and optional RFID/NFC data.
  3. User removes or uses productWeight change tells the system that a product was lifted, used, or returned.
  4. System confirms through haptic/audio feedbackShort vibration and voice cues confirm the product or guide the user back to the correct slot.
  5. System tracks usage and alerts when neededRemaining quantity, misplacement, and safety events can trigger timely reminders or alerts.

Launch Roadmap

From prototype to accessible bathroom ecosystem.

The roadmap follows a design thinking and product development path: validate the problem, test the physical interaction, build the sensing system, trial it with users, and pilot it in real bathroom environments.

Phase 01 Research validation

Interviews, user journey mapping, and problem framing with visually impaired users, caregivers, and accessible-space stakeholders.

Expected outcomeClear problem statement, prioritized user needs, and validated routine pain points.
What will be testedWhether product confusion, privacy loss, and wet-environment difficulty are the right design focus.
Phase 02 Low-fidelity prototype

Cardboard, foam, or 3D layout models to explore tray size, slot spacing, tactile affordances, and reach patterns.

Expected outcomeA physical layout users can touch, critique, and compare before electronics are added.
What will be testedSlot logic, product placement, hand movement, tactile discoverability, and counter footprint.
Phase 03 Functional prototype

Sensor-enabled dock with controller logic, weight or identity sensing, haptic motor, and audio feedback.

Expected outcomeA working proof-of-concept that can recognize product interaction and provide non-visual confirmation.
What will be testedSensor accuracy, feedback timing, battery behavior, waterproofing approach, and low-product logic.
Phase 04 User testing

Trials with visually impaired users and caregivers, including wet-hand, noisy-room, and steamy-environment scenarios.

Expected outcomeEvidence on usability, trust, comfort, privacy impact, and where feedback needs refinement.
What will be testedProduct identification, misplaced-product alerts, haptic/audio clarity, and safety-sensing acceptability.
Phase 05 Pilot deployment

Install pilots in homes, assisted-living spaces, and accessible hotel bathrooms to test the product in real routines.

Expected outcomeDeployment learnings, installation requirements, service model, and pilot evidence for adoption.
What will be testedDurability, cleaning, battery life, replenishment workflows, caregiver alerts, and long-term usefulness.
